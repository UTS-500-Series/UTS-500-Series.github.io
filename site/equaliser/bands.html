The circuit — 03

The parametric bands

Each band is a state-variable filter wired as a peaking equaliser: four op amps, one quad package, and three knobs — frequency, width and boost/cut. The two bands are the same circuit; only the two integrator capacitors differ.

Every figure here is worked out from the schematic’s netlist with linear pots and ideal op amps (how). Q is the centre frequency over the width between the points at half the peak’s height in dB. With the boost/cut knob centred, each band is flat to within 0.01 dB from 10 Hz to 100 kHz. The two frequency ranges overlap slightly, between 1.05 kHz and 1.26 kHz, so between them they cover 191 Hz to 6.92 kHz with no gap.

How a band works

A state-variable filter is a summing amplifier followed by two integrators in a loop. The summer (unit A in the low-mid band) produces a high-pass output, the first integrator (B) a band-pass, the second (C) a low-pass, and the low-pass is fed back to the summer to close the loop. The reason it is used for parametric EQ is that its centre frequency and its width are set by different parts, so one knob can move without disturbing the other.

Frequency

Each integrator’s time constant is its 10k input resistor times its capacitor, so the top of each range is 1 / (2π × 10k × C): 1.25 kHz for 12.7 nF and 6.9 kHz for 2.3 nF. Turning RV9 down taps the signal lower on a 10k track that ends in 2k2, which scales down what reaches the integrator. At the bottom of the track the integrator sees only 2.2/12.2 of the signal through about 11.8k, the same as a 65k resistor, so the range spans about 6.5 to 1. Both gangs move together, which is what keeps the width constant as the frequency changes.

Boost and cut

The output stage (IC3D) takes the dry signal into its − input through R29, with R39 as feedback, and the band-pass output into its + input through C7. With R29 and R39 equal, the dry signal comes out at a gain of −1, plus the band-pass. RV8 sits between the band’s input (pin 1) and its output (pin 3), and its wiper is what the filter listens to.

  • Wiper at the input end: the filter hears the input, and its band-pass is added to the output. The band boosts.
  • Wiper at the output end: the filter hears the output, so the band-pass inside the loop pulls its own band back down. The band cuts, by the same amount.
  • Wiper at centre: the input and the output are equal and opposite, so the wiper sits at zero and the filter hears nothing. The band is flat.

Because each band inverts, the two bands together come out in phase with the input. Any inverting stage added later (the gain stage is one) flips the module’s polarity, so count them before wiring J1’s balanced pins.

Checked against the team’s simulation

The low-mid band was simulated in LTspice on 30 September (Low_Bandpass_Filter/LT_SPICE/BandA.asc), with the frequency pot at the top of its range, the width pot centred and full boost. That run peaks at +9.52 dB at 1.26 kHz; the netlist calculation gives +9.54 dB at 1.26 kHz for the same settings, and the dots on the chart lie on the calculated curve. Two different methods, one using TI’s OPA1644 model and one ideal op amps, agree — which says the KiCad sheet and the LTspice file describe the same circuit.
