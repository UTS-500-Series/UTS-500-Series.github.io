The circuit — 05

The shelves

A low shelf and a high shelf, built from the high-pass, low-pass and gain circuits the team first drew on separate sheets. Each filter picks out the frequencies its shelf acts on, a level stage sets how much, and a summer adds the result to the dry signal or takes it away.

How a shelf is made

After the two bands, buffer IC4B feeds three paths at once:

  • Dry: IC8D inverts the signal (R44 and R49, both 10k).
  • Low shelf: IC4D low-pass filters it. The filtered signal goes to the summer directly through R54, and through the level stage IC8A, which inverts it, through R52.
  • High shelf: the same, with IC4C high-pass filtering, the level stage IC8B, and R53 and R51.

The summer, IC8C, adds the five inputs with its 10k feedback resistor R55. The dry signal comes in through 10k, so it reaches the output at unity, inverted back the right way up. Each filter comes in twice through 16k: once directly, and once through its level stage, inverted. With a level stage at a gain of exactly 1, those two copies are equal and opposite and cancel, and the shelf does nothing. Turn the level knob up and the inverted copy wins, so the filtered band is added to the dry signal: boost. Turn it down and the direct copy wins, which subtracts: cut.

The level stages

Each level stage is the team’s variable-gain circuit: R45 (4.7k), the 10k track of RV3 and R46 (4.7k) in series from input to output, with the wiper on the op amp’s − input. The gain is the resistance on the output side of the wiper over the resistance on the input side, from 4.7k/14.7k (−9.9 dB) to 14.7k/4.7k (+9.9 dB), and exactly 1 at the centre. That is the circuit from the gain sheet with its inputs the right way round.

Both filters now do what the separate sheets were reaching for. The low-pass has its capacitor to ground, and a dual-gang pot moves both resistors together, so the corner sweeps cleanly instead of being lowest in the middle of the knob’s travel. The ranges that looked odd for a high-pass and a low-pass — a high-pass at 4.5–20 kHz, a low-pass below 1.3 kHz — make sense for shelves: they pick out the top and bottom of the audio band, which is what a shelf lifts or lowers.

The corner is where the shelf has reached half its depth in dB. With its corner at the bottom of the range the low shelf hasn’t fully arrived by 20 Hz, which is why its cut reads less there.

Three things the response shows

  • The high shelf is weaker and lopsided. It reaches about +7.0 dB / −4.2 dB where the low shelf reaches ±9.8 dB, and with its corner at the top of the range it barely acts below 20 kHz. Its path has the R12/R13 divider (×0.64) ahead of the filter, which the low shelf’s doesn’t, so its two copies arrive at the summer smaller. If the two shelves should match, that divider is the place to look.
  • A dip beside each shelf. Boosting the low shelf dips the response by about 3 dB just above the corner, and cutting it bumps it by about 1.4 dB; the high shelf does the same below its corner by 1–2 dB. That comes from adding a second-order (Q 0.71) filter to the dry signal. Some classic equalisers have this on purpose. A lower-Q filter would make it smaller.
  • C33 tilts the flat response. With every knob centred the module rises to +0.5 dB around 12 kHz. C33 (220 pF across the high shelf’s level stage) rolls off the inverted copy of the high-passed signal but not the direct one, so they stop cancelling at the top of the band. At 22 pF the module is flat to 0.05 dB. The board is ordered with 220 pF, so fitting 22 pF is a build-time choice, not a board change. C29 on the low shelf does the same thing in principle, but there is almost no low-passed signal up where it acts.
