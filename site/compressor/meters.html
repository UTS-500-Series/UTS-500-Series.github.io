Sheet 7

The LED meters

Two ten-segment meters — gain reduction and output level — driven by one NE5532 and a pair of LM3914 display drivers. Both light exactly one LED at a time, which is the only reason they fit the power budget at all.

Why this sheet uses a driver IC

A ten-segment meter needs ten comparators, and there are two of them. Building twenty comparators out of NE5532s means ten more packages and roughly 80 mA — on rails that only have about 60 mA of headroom left. It does not fit.

So this is the one place the module steps outside its NE5532-and-BC549 palette. U9 and U10 are LM3914 display drivers: ten comparators, a reference and the LED current sinks in one 18-pin package. The LM3914's ten steps are evenly spaced in volts.

The level meter was first drawn with an LM3915, the same chip with 3 dB steps. Nobody stocks it any more, so both meters now use the LM3914. The gain-reduction meter suits it anyway, and the level meter's scale is set out below.

Dot mode, not bar mode

Pin 9 (MODE) is left open on both drivers, which selects dot mode: one LED lit at a time instead of a filled bar. That is a power decision before it is an aesthetic one. In bar mode, ten lit LEDs per meter at about 10 mA would be 200 mA and the module would be far over the 130 mA the rack allows. In dot mode the two meters cost about 22 mA.

The LED current is set by the driver, not by a series resistor per LED, so all twenty segments match without sorting parts. RHI ties to REFOUT and RLO to ground, so each ladder runs from 0 V to its full scale in ten equal steps.

R92 was 8k2 like R90. With ten LEDs the level meter's top LED is the full-scale voltage itself, and at 5.05 V even RV8 turned fully up could not bring it below +19.3 dBu. At 4k7 the top LED sits at +18 dBu with RV8 about a fifth of the way round.

Gain reduction — U9

The control voltage CTRL-B rests at 0 V and swings negative as the module compresses: about −10 V by the design, −8 V at the most reduction in simulation. It needs inverting before a meter can read it. That much is easy. The problem is what happens next.

Q6 and Q7 in the gain cell are an undegenerated differential pair, so gain follows control voltage as a sigmoid, not a line. Feed that straight to a meter that steps evenly in volts and the first few segments all land inside the first couple of dB, while the last ones need more reduction than the module can give.

R93 (18 kΩ) solves it by making U7B a summing inverting stage: it subtracts a fixed offset taken from U9's own reference before the signal reaches the driver. The even voltage steps then land on the useful part of the sigmoid. With RV7 set for a top LED at 30 dB, the simulated scale is:

Output level — U10

The level meter watches OUT-A, the makeup amplifier's output. That is the last point in the signal path that is still a low-impedance op-amp output: metering after R28 would put the meter's input divider across the module's balanced output and unbalance it slightly.

U7A is a precision peak detector. D11 sits inside the feedback loop, so the op amp servos out the diode's own forward drop and the detector stays accurate down to a few millivolts — a bare diode and capacitor would read about 0.6 V low. R87 limits the surge into C35 as it charges.

Simulated

The simulation's LM3914 model has the reference, the divider, all ten comparators, dot mode and the LED current sinks, so every LED's current is a result rather than an assumption. On a +20 dBu burst the level meter touches its top LED for the first 15 ms or so, until the attack catches up, and the gain-reduction meter climbs to D26 and steps back down over the release. The simulation page has the method.

Setting the two trimmers

Both meters have a trimmer, and both want a signal generator and a voltmeter.

  • RV8, level. Feed the module a steady tone at whatever level you want the top LED to mean — +18 dBu is the usual choice, leaving a few dB before the output clips. Adjust RV8 until D39 just lights. The rest of the scale then follows the table above.
  • RV7, gain reduction. Drive the compressor hard enough to produce a known amount of gain reduction, measured as the difference in output level with the threshold backed off and applied. Adjust RV7 until the segment you want lights: D29 at 30 dB puts the rest where the table above says. Set it against a measured CTRL-B, not the design's −10 V.

The LED current in the model counts the driver's internal divider as well as R89/R91, so if anything it reads a little high; this is still the one number in the project worth measuring on the bench.

The LM3914 is end of life

Checked at DigiKey on 2 September 2026: LM3914N-1/NOPB and LM3915N-1/NOPB are obsolete with no stock — and so is every other dot/bar display driver they list. The dedicated analogue bargraph driver is an extinct product category, not a part that happens to be out of stock.

Altronics still lists the LM3914 (Z2670, low stock, held at its Cannington store when checked on 3 October 2026), and both meters now use it, so order the two early. Rochester Electronics, an authorised seller of obsolete TI parts, is the place to look if it runs out.

If it does, a comparator ladder is the fallback: five LM339 quads cover twenty thresholds and are stocked for cents each. What the LM3914 actually buys is its dot-mode logic and its constant-current sinks — a ladder needs a resistor per LED, a divider chain to set the thresholds and extra steering if dot mode is to be kept. Only this sheet is affected.

Still open

Nothing here has been built. The gain-reduction scale comes from a model of the steering pair, not from measurement — the shape is right, but expect to move RV7 and to redraw the panel legend once a real one exists. LED1 on sheet 6 is superseded by this sheet and has no hole in the panel.
